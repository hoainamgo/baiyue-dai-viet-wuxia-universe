# DRAGON SYMBOLS AND THE HEAVENLY WORLDVIEW

> **Public Document · Updated 2026-09-25**
> [`57_CANON_DONG_A_THAI_THOI_TRAN_1225_1400.md`](57_CANON_DONG_A_THAI_THOI_TRAN_1225_1400.md) is the preferred standard when different.

## 1. Purpose

The document examines two separate questions:

1. How were dragons used in Chinese dynasties?
2. How can “Holy Respect” be used in the Dai Viet worldview without turning the interpretation of divine theory into an absolute historical conclusion?

## 2. Dragon in Chinese history

### 2.1. Do not assign an absolute starting point

- Dragon images have layers of ancient meaning, appearing in many regions and contexts before and after the Han Dynasty.
- The record *Sima Qian's History* about Liu Bang and Liu Bang's mother needs to be analyzed as a posthumous document, not automatically proving that "Liu Bang fabricated it" during his lifetime.
- The right to use dragon images, colors, number of claws and rituals changed according to the dynasty. Must cite laws, main dictionaries, inscriptions or research specifically for each landmark.

### 2.2. Safe presentation

| Sentences to use | Sentences should not be used |
|---|---|
| “Records of Chinese records of the legend of the Son of Heaven associated with the dragon.” | “Since 202 BC, all peoples can only use five-clawed dragons.” |
| “The following dynasties increased control over the dragon symbol according to each regulation.” | “The Han Dynasty created an entire dragon culture and punished the common people absolutely.” |
| “Yellow, dragon robes and robes symbolize both power and divinity.” | “Dragons are just tools of oppression, with absolutely no positive meaning.” |

### 2.3. Boundary with Dai Viet

- Dragons are a symbol system with its own history; does not automatically become Giao Long of Dong Son.
- When Dai Viet architecture has dragon motifs, the name of the monument, period, materials and source must be recorded.
- The main worldview does not import the Northern dragon as a flying weapon, magic or primary power.

## 3. "Respecting God" in worldview

### 3.1. Worldview structure

In stories and images, characters can build open-air stone altars, offer clear spring water, new rice, or offerings associated with their profession; The oath under the sky creates resonance with rain, harvest and community.

Unified rules:

1. The open altar is a symbol of the worldview of Sacrifice to Heaven, not a statement that every historical community from time to time had the same unchanging rituals.
2. Spring water, new rice, sticky rice seeds, fruit or sticky rice wine may appear according to specific rituals; Do not lock down a single set of offerings.
3. Incense, smoke, statues, pagodas, shrines and modern rituals can be examined as historical phenomena; do not assume they are the behavior of the worldview character.
4. Truc Lam Zen is a history class of the Tran Dynasty, not synonymous with "absolute original beliefs".

### 3.2. Relationship with folk beliefs

- Proverbs about Heaven, Earth, filial piety, duty and justice can serve as a source of cultural inspiration.
- It is not confirmed that all ancient rituals were directly derived from the Noah legend or from a "primitive monotheistic belief".
- When using theodicy, it must be clearly stated that it is an interpretive layer, not a replacement for historical religion.
- Do not create violence, racism or deification of people in your worldview.

## 4. Used for storytelling

- Characters under the sky can offer sacrifices, keep oaths, or choose justice through actions, not through divine declarations.
- The conflict between power and oath becomes dramatic: kings, generals, captains, workers and dike builders can all swear or betray their oaths.
- The open-air altar is a place where the community defines values, not a divine altar with automatic punishment.
