## 🗺️ SECTION 1: NORTHERN COASTAL SALT REGION & LOCATION REQUIRED / IMMEDIATELY

- **[TRUE HISTORY]** Right next to the 13th century, *Dai Viet Complete History* (The Chronicles, volume IV) recorded the year **Ky Mui 1179** of Ly Cao Tong's reign: *"In June, a decree prohibited salt and iron products from being sold and exchanged at the upstream."* (Vietnamese history brief:禁魚鹽鐵器不得至源頭博易). Just this one sentence is enough to prove that sea salt is a **sovereign commodity under state management** since before the Tran dynasty: the state had to ban its flow.
- **[REAL HISTORY]** *An Nam chi comb* (Le Tac, 14th century) wrote about Dai Viet products: boiling sea water to get salt, white as snow; People from the border areas followed because of the benefits of salt and iron. This is the only ancient source that directly states the **method (cooking) and color (snow white)** of contemporary salt.
- **[TRUE HISTORY]** Origin of the Tran family according to *Toan Thu*: *"Someone named Kinh came to Tuc Mac village, Thien Truong district, and gave birth to Ham; Ham gave birth to Ly"* - forever **fishing** on the river. Tuc Mac is therefore a **river field**, not a rice field.
- **[CONTROLLED INTERPRETATION]** Regarding **Can Boi / Tu Mac (Kinh Mon, Hai Duong)**: looking up ancient geographs shows a difference that needs to be clearly stated - *Tuc Mac* classic is incense belonging to Thien Truong district (now Nam Dinh); Meanwhile, the **King Mon (Hai Duong)** region has a near-modern place **Can Boi commune, Tu Mac district, Kinh Mon district, Hai Duong town** (Tu Mac now belongs to Minh Tan ward, Kinh Mon town). I **could not find** any deed in *An Nam Chi comb, Dai Nam Nhat Thong Chi* part of Hai Duong calling Can Boi the royal salt flat. Therefore, this SSOT **convention**: Can Boi/Tu Mac was built as a **salt farm on the outskirts of the Eastern region** - located at the Kinh Mon gateway, where the Kinh Thay - Da Vach river flows into the tidal strip along the Northeast coast, connecting with the larger salt flats of Hai Phong - Do Son today. This is a **story convention**, not a historical event.
- **[REAL HISTORY]** Kinh Mon terrain is recognized as an area with a system of Kinh Thay river, Da Vach river, Han Mau river flowing around, interspersed with limestone mountains (An Phu, Kinh Chu - Duong Nham cave). Meaning: **salt here comes by boat on the river, not by vehicle on land** — this detail is real and can be used immediately.
- **Story writing point:** Don't call Can Boi the "salt capital" — call it **"the salt farm at the end of the beach, where the salt water encroaches on the foot of the limestone mountain"**; the setting is where the salty mud meets the cliff.

## 🧂 SECTION 2: METHOD OF SALT MAKING — MEDIEVAL REFERENCE

- **[TRUE HISTORY]** Two salt making routes in the medieval world, both have evidence: **(a) sun drying and crystallization** - bringing sea water into the field, condensing with sun and wind for salt to crystallize; **(b) boil salt water** — cook sea water/salt water in a shallow pan. *An Nam chi comb* copy branches (b): boil sea water to get salt as white as snow. The traditional Vietnamese salt profession is still alive today (coastal salt fields, typical of Hai Phong - Do Son area, Bang La communes used to have Tien Phong, Hong Phong, Tan Phong salt cooperatives) keeping branch (a): sea water is brought into the fields, filtered through sand, dried in the sun to concentrate, raked into beds.
- **[TRUE HISTORY]** In Dai Viet, the word **match (鹽)** is salt; same common route: **raw salt water → filtration → concentration → crystallization → collection**. I do not attribute to the 13th century any machinery or equipment that does not appear in historical records.
- **[CONTROLLED IMAGINATION]** Specific stages of operating Can Boi salt flats (based on living occupations, no 13th century documents):
 1. **Collecting water:** Open the creek gate to welcome tide water into the outside field (drying field), keep it for a few days to let the mud settle and the sun drain away the fresh water.
 2. **Sprinkling salt water on the sand:** sprinkle salt water on the sand of the drying yard so that the salt sticks to the sand grains (this is the origin of the phrase "drilling salt water, sifting sand" in the ancient profession).
 3. **Sifting:** Salty sand is sifted and washed with salt water, to obtain **clear salt water, heavier than** normal sea water.
 4. **Crystallization:** That salty water flows down into the crystallized field as smooth as pounded mud; The sun dries it out, and the salt forms a thin layer on the surface of the mud.
 5. **Raking & cultivating:** raking the salt into a white bed, draining it, and carrying it onto the beach.
 6. **Assisting with fire:** On days when the weather is cloudy and the salt water does not have enough sunlight, it is boiled in a **pan/cooking pot** (in accordance with the words of *An Nam chi chop* about cooking sea water). Firewood is expensive, so fire is only used when stuck — this detail creates great economic drama.
- **Story point:** The energy of the scene is **sunshine**, not fire; Fire (pan) is a sign of the rainy season, the hungry season, or the salt owners are forcing people to bear debt.

## 🌗 SECTION 3: LIFE CYCLE ACCORDING TO THE TIDE

- **[REAL HISTORY]** The **diurnal / diurnal tide** mechanism in the North Sea: the Northern coastal area mainly has water rising and falling **once** a day (different from the semi-diurnal tide twice a day elsewhere); Salt people live by the water, fetching water when the tide is high and drying the fields when the tide recedes. This is an astronomical - hydrological truth, and can be used without citation.
- **[REAL HISTORY]** The tidal regime in the Gulf of Tonkin is **irregular diurnal tide** (one rise and fall every day, sometimes with a small rhythm), strongly influenced by the monsoon: the northeast monsoon makes the water rise, in the summer the south-southwest wind makes the water weaker. The high tide cycle follows the phases of the moon (new moon, full moon).
- **[CONTROLLED THOUGHT]** Can Boi beach calendar during the year: **salt season runs from December to March (lunar calendar)** — dry, north wind, hot sun, water receding; **April - August** Heavy rain, freshwater discharge, salt fields "die", salt workers switch to fishing, weaving nets, repairing field edges. A working day begins at **water receding**, not at sunrise: keeping time is made by **the shadow of bamboo poles and the sound of water**, not by temple bells.
- **[CONTROLLED THOUGHT]** Specific rhythm: listening to the water rising at night - opening the creek in the morning - drying at noon - raking in the afternoon - carrying it back to the warehouse at night; **On a moonlit day (high tide)**, he stayed up all night to guard the shore, because a huge flood swept away a month's work.
- **Story writing point:** The salt farm's clock is a **water meter**, so the power struggle is also calculated by the water meter: the one who holds the creek mouth is the one who controls the whole village.

## 🛠️ SECTION 4: SALT BEACH TOOLS

- **[TRUE HISTORY]** Only these **materials and principles** are proven: bamboo, wood, terracotta - clay pots (Vietnamese ceramics have a long tradition, ash-glazed earthenware jars - eel skin), stone, wooden stakes in the beach (the technique of planting ironwood, sycamore, and clay stakes under tidal mud has been proven archaeologically at the Bach Dang stake yard, 13th century). Pan/pot to boil salt water: *An Nam chi comb* records the practice of cooking sea water.
- **[CONTROLLED THOUGHTS]** Five tools of the yard (named briefly, described according to living occupation, **not recognized as archaeological artifacts**):
 1. **Water sieve / sand sieve** — sieve, woven bamboo sieve, thin slats, to filter salty sand and trash; After sifting for a long time, the bamboo will turn white because of the salt it eats.
 2. **Water wheel** — bamboo/wooden wheel placed at the mouth of the creek, taking advantage of rising and falling tides to bring salt water into the field without carrying each bucket; The person standing on the reel must watch the water. If he slips, his arm will be broken.
 3. **Salt rake** — wide wooden rake, long bamboo handle, pulled over crystallized field surface; The rake blade is worn out into a crescent shape.
 4. **Roll stakes / boundary marker stakes** — bamboo stakes, wooden stakes stuck in the mud to mark the fields, markers for the owner and worker, and a string to measure salinity by dropping an egg or potato (floating = salty enough).
 5. **Buried clay jars** — clay pots and jars (ash glaze, eel skin) buried half under the floor of the warehouse to keep the salt from getting wet; The jar's mouth is covered with dry banana leaves and plastered with mud; That type of salt is called **reserve salt**, only touched when there are guests, groups, or soldiers.
- **Story writing point:** Each tool must have **a unique mark**: a worn sieve, a rake handle shiny with sweat, a piece of the jar's mouth - torn props are characters with a past.

## 👥 SECTION 5: SOCIAL CLASS IN A SALT FARM

- **[REAL HISTORY]** Dai Viet's rural structure during the Ly - Tran dynasties was **self-governing villages and communes** with a class of civil servants, landlords, and tenants; The *Complete Book* of 1203 (Ly Cao Tong) recorded the establishment of commune management positions, showing that the state had reached out to the village and commune levels. The names of translated positions in the corpus (State Chief, Ward Chief...) are **conventions of the series**, not Tran Dynasty officials.
- **[TRUE HISTORY]** During the Tran Dynasty, there were various types of **rent, land tax, nail tax, and forced labor**; *salt* is in the group of products that are beneficial to the state (testimony: the ban on bringing salt to the upstream in 1179; the words of *An Nam chi strate* about the benefits of salt - iron).
- **[CONTROLLED REFLECTION]** Four floors of Can Boi camp:
 - **Saltman (saltman):** a person who carries water, rakes fields, and carries salt; burnt skin, white shoulders with salt patches; work according to the work, eat meals according to the master; owe the master with salt, pay with salt, die and still owe salt.
 - **Salt owner:** the person who keeps the crystallization fields and storage jars; calculate by **water and debt**, not by blood relations; This person often also lends rice before the season.
 - **Salt merchant:** people connecting the beach with the market; buy cheap salt when the season is good, transport it up Kinh Thay river to Eastern markets, exchange rice, linen, and dried fish; The bamboo boat is woven from bamboo shoots, plastered with bottle - buffalo dung - sawdust to prevent salt water (according to the description of the bamboo boat in the corpus).
 - **Bao chief:** the person standing in the middle - who takes care of village affairs, is also the tax paying arm, and is the one who redistributes the fields each season when the water recedes; The real power of the beach lies in this hand, not in the salt owner.
- **Story writing point:** The best conflict of the salt flats is not the owner - the worker, but **the yard captain vs the merchant boat**: one side keeps the fields, the other keeps the price.

## ⚖️ SECTION 6: SALT TAX & TRENDY MONOPOLY

- **[REAL HISTORY]** The only contemporary evidence is the **ban in 1179**: *prohibiting fish sauce, salt, and iron from being sold and exchanged at the source*. Implication: the state considers salt and iron to be **strategic goods**, controlled by location and direction of flow. This is the seed of the salt management policy that we have the right to call **fashionable monopoly** — but **it is not allowed** to say that Ly/Tran established any "salt company" or "salt department", because there is no document.
- **[True HISTORY]** *An Nam chi luc* recorded salt and iron as two benefits that made the border people serve - meaning the state held the resources, the people held the labor. That structure was the core of the original salt tax: **taxed on people, not on goods**.
- **[CONTROLLED THOUGHT]** Collection method at Can Boi beach (constructed, no historical data): collected by **salt in kind according to the number of earthen jars / number of burdens raked**, recorded on bamboo cards; During the bad season, it turned into working to build the banks, pull boats, and transport soldiers. Merchant boats that want to leave the creek mouth must have a **mud-stamped bamboo card** from the yard chief; lost card = that salt is illegal salt.
- **Story point:** The salt tax does not appear as a brocade-clad mandarin; It appeared in the form of **a bamboo tag stuck in the bow of the boat**, and a salt farmer looked at it and knew what his family would eat for dinner this month.

## 🔥 SECTION 7: SALT VILLAGE LANGUAGE — FIVE SENSES & MEALS

*(Background: dai_toan_thu_phong_tho_sinh_hoat_dai_viet.md — folk cuisine, black/indigo clothes, going barefoot, singing and answering by the water)*

- **[REAL HISTORY]** Material weather borrowed from background literature: four-piece shirt, blouse **coarse linen/dyed with brown, indigo roots**; the habit of **walking barefoot** (calloused feet cling firmly to the mud); **earthen pot, bamboo chopsticks, bamboo bed, sedge mat**; folk meal: **braised goby with Ban soy sauce, boiled water spinach dipped in ginger soy sauce, crab soup with spinach, salted eggplant compressed in eel skin clay pot, rice balls with sesame salt**; Drinks: **robust water, braised green tea with accumulated straw**.
- **[CONTROLLED THOUGHT]** Transformation to the salt village:
 - **White shoulder salt shirt:** The linen absorbs salty sweat, dries to a **moldy white border on the shoulders and back of the neck**; Beachgoers recognize each other by the salt streaks on their backs, without having to look at each other's faces.
 - **Sunburn:** not described in general — describes **salt rubbed into cracks in heels and between fingers**, burning like a bee sting; Apply fish oil or green banana sap to soften.
 - **Saline wells:** wells in the village can only get brackish water; Fresh water must be transported by boat from the upper reaches of the Kinh Thay River, measured in **coconut shells**; A bucket of fresh water can be exchanged for a large handful of salt — this is the village's biggest luxury.
 - **Salted rice meal:** red rice stuffed with yam, braised goby with soy sauce cooked the day before to prevent rancidity, boiled water spinach dipped in salt; Eat on a bamboo mat on the porch to escape the wind; Drink a bowl of water to cool down.
 - **Salt water chanting:** is not a Cheo song, but **a chant that accompanies the rhythm of pulling the rake and closing the creek door** - one person chants, the whole field responds, the verse is short, rhythmic to the footsteps of wading through the mud; On nights when the tide is high, voices echo from field to field like an alarm.
- **Story writing point:** The most expensive detail of the salt village is the **salt it leaves on things**: on the brim of the hat, on the rake handle, on the mouth of the jar, on the hair; When the character leaves the salt flat, the **salty smell still follows for three days**.
